Introduction / Background

The applications of machine learning in supply chain analytics has gained significant momentum, offering logistics managers, analysts, and organizations actionable insights to improve operational efficiency.

In supply chain management, understanding and optimizing delivery processes, shipment planning, and delivery risk is critical for customer satisfaction and business success. Traditional approaches often rely on historical analysis and manual judgements, which may lead to suboptimal decisions and delayed identification of high-risk shipments.

✅ Literature Review

  • Recent studies have applied machine learning techniques to address challenges in supply chain management, such as predicting delivery delays and optimizing shipment planning.
  • Supervised learning techniques, including Logistic Regression, Random Forest and CatBoost have been used to predict late delivery risk using historical order, customer, product, shipping and geographical data.
  • Explainable AI techniques, particularly SHAP, have been used to identify the key factors influencing delivery risk predictions and provide interpretable insights into individual shipments.
  • These approaches help automate supply chain analysis, providing data-driven insights to identify high-risk deliveries, improve logistics planning and support better operational decisions.

Problem Definition

✅ Problem

Supply chain operations involve various shipping methods, customers, products and delivery routes. However, predicting which orders are likely to be delivered late can be a very difficult task.

Rather than using a data-driven approach to predict delivery risk, organizations may rely on historical analysis and subjective judgements that can lead to poor logistics planning and customer dissatisfaction.

✅ Motivation

Automating the analysis of supply chain orders to identify potential delivery delays can allow organizations to make better and more strategic operational decisions.

Using machine learning models to predict late delivery risk and explain the factors influencing each prediction, this project provides insights to help organizations identify high-risk shipments, improve logistics planning and optimize delivery operations.

Methods

Data Processing Methods

1. Data Collection and Preparation

  • Data Source: Focused on the DataCo Smart Supply Chain dataset containing historical order and delivery information.
  • Data Loading: Using Pandas, the CSV dataset containing 180,519 orders and 53 features was loaded.
  • Data Cleaning: Performed data quality checks for missing values, duplicate records, and irrelevant or sensitive attributes before preparing the dataset for modelling.
  • Feature Selection: Selected 28 relevant features that could be used to predict delivery risk while excluding post-outcome variables such as actual shipping duration, delivery status and the target variable.

2. Feature Engineering

  • Shipping Features: Shipping mode, scheduled shipping days, order type and order item quantity.
  • Product and Order Features: Product category, department, product name, product price, discounts, discount rates and item price.
  • Customer Features: Customer segment, customer city, customer state and customer country.
  • Geographic Features: Order city, order state, order country, order region, market, latitude and longitude.
  • Temporal Features: Year, month, quarter, day of week, week number and weekend indicator.

3. Late Delivery Risk Prediction

  • Used Late_delivery_risk as the target variable.
  • 1 represents a late delivery and 0 represents an on-time delivery.
  • The processed data was divided into 80% training and 20% testing sets using stratified sampling to maintain the original class distribution.

Data Preprocessing

Leakage Prevention: Post-outcome variables such as actual shipping duration, delivery status, shipping date and the target variable were excluded from the prediction feature set.

The dataset was examined for missing values, duplicate records, irrelevant variables and inconsistent information before modelling.

Machine Learning Algorithms

Supervised Learning

Logistic Regression

Objective: Predict whether an order is likely to experience a late delivery based on shipping, customer, product, geographic and order-related characteristics.

  • Used as the baseline classification model.
  • Categorical features were encoded and numerical features processed before training.
  • Generated classifications and probability estimates.
  • Provided an interpretable baseline for binary classification.

Random Forest

Objective: Predict late delivery risk by capturing non-linear relationships and complex interactions between supply chain features.

  • Used the same selected feature set as Logistic Regression.
  • Categorical variables were encoded into numerical representations.
  • Used multiple decision trees to learn patterns associated with late deliveries.
  • Generated classifications and probability estimates.

CatBoost

Objective: Develop a high-performing late delivery prediction model capable of effectively handling the large number of categorical variables present in the supply chain dataset.

  • CatBoost directly handled categorical variables such as shipping mode, customer city, order city, product name and customer segment.
  • The model was trained on the prepared training data.
  • It generated late-delivery probabilities and classifications.
  • CatBoost achieved the strongest performance and was selected as the final prediction model.

Rationale for Model and Method Selection

Logistic Regression

  • Interpretability: Clear relationships between input features and late-delivery probability.
  • Efficiency: Computationally efficient baseline.
  • Probabilistic Outputs: Provides probability estimates for assessing shipment risk.

Random Forest

  • Non-Linear Relationships: Captures non-linear relationships and feature interactions.
  • Feature Importance: Provides feature importance scores.
  • Robustness: Combines multiple decision trees through an ensemble structure.

CatBoost

  • Categorical Data Handling: Suitable for datasets containing many categorical variables.
  • Non-Linear Relationships: Captures complex interactions between supply-chain features.
  • Performance: Produced the best results among the evaluated models.
  • Reduced Preprocessing: Processes categorical variables directly, reducing the need for extensive one-hot encoding.

Results and Discussion

Exploratory Data Analysis — Key Drivers of Delay

Overall late-delivery rate: 54.83% of all orders in the dataset were late.

Shipping mode showed the strongest observed relationship with late-delivery risk. First Class shipments had a 95.3% observed late rate, followed by Second Class at 76.6%, Same Day at 45.7%, and Standard Class at 38.1%.

Scheduled Shipping Duration

Scheduled shipping duration followed a similar pattern. A one-day scheduled window showed a 95.3% observed late rate compared with 38.1% for a four-day window.

Market and Customer Segment

Market and Customer Segment showed relatively modest variation. Late-delivery risk ranged from approximately 54.4% to 55.2% across markets and from approximately 54.7% to 55.1% across customer segments.

Supervised Learning Performance Metrics

CatBoost decisively outperformed both baselines across every reported metric.

The Logistic Regression baseline achieved 71.16% accuracy and a 77.91% ROC-AUC. Random Forest achieved 69.22% accuracy and a 76.30% ROC-AUC.

CatBoost achieved 92.03% accuracy, 95.37% precision, 89.83% recall, 92.51% F1 score and 98.21% ROC-AUC on the stratified held-out test set.

Why CatBoost Performed Better

Random Forest required one-hot encoding, expanding the categorical feature space to approximately 5,041 columns. CatBoost's native handling of categorical fields allowed it to exploit high-cardinality features such as Customer City and Order City more effectively.

Individual Explanation

At the individual-order level, SHAP waterfall explanations allow the model to justify a specific prediction feature-by-feature.

For example, one sampled test order was predicted to have a 59.4% probability of being late, corresponding to the MEDIUM risk tier.

The prediction was primarily influenced by a negative risk-reducing contribution from its Standard Class shipping mode (SHAP -0.90), partially offset by positive contributions from customer-state and order-region values.

Interpretability: SHAP makes it possible to audit individual predictions instead of treating the machine learning model as a black box.

Conclusion

The implemented solution combines data processing, feature engineering and supervised learning to analyse and predict delivery performance using historical supply chain data.

By using relevant shipping, customer, product, geographic and temporal features, the analysis identifies patterns associated with late delivery risk.

Logistic Regression served as a reliable baseline, while Random Forest was used to capture non-linear relationships and complex feature interactions.

CatBoost demonstrated superior performance by effectively handling the large number of categorical features present in the dataset.

Results and Discussions

Supervised Learning Quantitative Metrics

  2. Precision: The precision of 95.37% indicates that most orders predicted as late were actually late.
  3. Recall: A recall of 89.83% indicates that the model identified a large proportion of the actual late deliveries.
