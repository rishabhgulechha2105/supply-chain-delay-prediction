🚚 Supply Chain Delay Prediction & Logistics Risk Intelligence

Introduction/Background

The applications of machine learning in supply chain analytics has gained significant momentum, offering logistics managers, analysts, and organizations actionable insights to improve operational efficiency. In supply chain management, understanding and optimizing delivery processes, shipment planning, and delivery risk is critical for customer satisfaction and business section. Traditional approaches often rely on historical analysis and manual judgements, which may lead to suboptimal decisions and delayed identification of high risk shipments.

✅ Literature Review

  • Recent studies have applied machine learning techniques to address challenges in supply chain management, such as predicting delivery delays and optimizing shipment planning.

  • Supervised learning techniques, including Logistic Regression, Random Forest and Cat Boost have been used to predict late delivery risk using historical order, customer, product, shipping and geographical data.

  • Explainable AI techniques particularly SHAP have been used to identify the key factors influencing delivery risk predictions and provide interpretable insights into individual shipments.

  • These approaches help automate supply chain analysis, providing data driven insights to identify high risk deliveries, improve logistics planning and support better operational decisions.

✅ Dataset Description

  • The data is provided as a CSV file containing historical supply chain and order information from the DataCo Smart Supply Chain dataset.

  • Order Data: Contains information about customer orders, including order type, quantity, discounts, prices, sales, and order details.

  • Shipping Data: Includes shipping models and scheduled shipping duration which are important for predicting delivery delays.

  • Customer Data: Contains customer related information’s such as customer segment, city, state and country.

  • Product Data: Provides details about products, categories , departments, product name and pricing information.

  • Geographic Data: Includes customer and order locations such as cities, states, countries, regions, markets, latitude and longitude.

  • Delivery Risk Data: The late_delivery_risk variable serves as the target, indicating whether an order is likely to experience a late delivery.

  • The dataset contains 180,519 historical orders and 53 features providing a comprehensive basis for developing and evaluating machine learning models for supply chain delay prediction.

Problem Definition

✅ Problem

Supply chain operations involve various shipping methods, customers, products, and delivery routes. However predicting which orders are likely to be delivered late can be a very difficult task. Rather than using a data driven approach to predict deliver risk organizations often rely on historical analysis and subjective judgements that can lead to poor logistics planning and customer dissatisfaction.

✅ Motivation

Automating the analysis of supply chain orders to identify potential delivery delays can allow the organizations to make better and strategic operational decisions. Using machine learning models to predict late delivery risk explain the factor influencing each prediction, this project will provide concrete insights to help organizations identify high risk shipments, improve logistics planning and optimize delivery operations. This in turn will help organizations better prepare for potential delays and also enhance supply chain performance.

Methods

Data Processing Methods

1. Data Collection and Preparation

  • Data Source: Focused on the DataCo Smart Supply Chain dataset containing historical order and delivery information.

  • Data Loading: Using Pandas we loaded the CSV dataset containing 180,519 orders and 53 features including customer, product, shipping, financial, and geographical information.

  • Data Cleaning: Performed data quality checks for missing values, duplicate records, and irrelevant or sensitive attributes before preparing he dataset for modelling.

  • Feature Selection: Selected 28 relevant features that could be used to predict delivery risk while excluding post outcome variables such as actual shipping duration, delivery status and the target variables.

2. Feature Engineering

  • Shipping Features: Used features such as shipping mode, scheduled shipping days, order type, and order item quantity to capture shipment and order characteristics.

  • Product and Order Features: Incorporated product category, department, product name, product name, product price, discounts, discount rates, and item price to represent product and transaction characteristics.

  • Customer Features: Customer segment, customer city, customer state, and customer country were included to capture customer related patterns.

  • Geographic Features: Order city, order state, order country, order region, market, latitude and longitude were used to capture geographic delivery patterns.

3. Late Delivery Risk Prediction

  • Used Late_delivery_risk as the target variable, where 1 represents a late delivery and 0 represents an on time delivery.

  • The processed data was divided into 80% training and 20% testing sets using stratified sampling to maintain the original class distribution.

Machine Learning Algorithms

Supervised Learning

Logistic Regression

  • Objective: The goal is to predict whether an order is likely to experience a late delivery based on its shipping, customer, product, geographic and order related characteristics.

  • Method:

  • Feature Selection: Selected 28 relevant features covering shipping, order, product, customer, geographic and temporal characteristics.

  • Data Preparation: Categorical features were encoded and numerical features were processed before training the model.

  • Model Training: Applied Logistic Regression to the training dataset to establish a baseline classification model.

  • Prediction: Used the trained model to classify orders as either Late or On Time and generate probability estimates.

Random Forest

  • Objective: To predict late delivery risk by capturing non-linear relationships and complex interactions between supply chain features.

  • Method:

  • Feature Selection: Used the same selected feature set as Logistic Regression to ensure a fair comparison.

  • Data Transformation: Categorical variables were encoded into numerical representations suitable for the Random Forest model.

  • Model Training: Applied a Random Forest classifier using multiple decision trees to learn patterns associated with late deliveries.

  • Prediction: Generated classifications and probability estimates for unseen orders and evaluated the model using accuracy, precision, recall, F1 Score and ROC-AUC.

  • Cat Boost

  • Objective: To develop a high performing late delivery prediction model capable of effectively handling the large number of categorical variables present in the supply chain dataset.

  • Method:

  • Feature Selection: Used shipping, order, product, customer, geographic and temporal features while excluding post outcome variables that cause data leakage.

  • Categorical Feature Handling: CatBoost directly handled categorical variables such as shipping mode, customer city, order city, product name and customer segment.

  • Model Training: Trained a CatBoost classification model on the prepared training data.

  • Prediction: Generated the probability of late delivery and classified each order as Late or On Time.

  • Model Selection: CatBoost achieved the strongest performance among the evaluated models and was selected as the final prediction model.

Rationale for Model and Method Selection

Logistic Regression

  • Interpretability: Logistic Regression provides clear and interpretable relationships between input features and the probability of late delivery.

  • Efficiency: It is computationally efficient and provides a strong baseline for binary classification.

  • Probabilistic Outputs: This model provides probability estimates for late delivery, which can be useful for assessing shipment risk.

Random Forest

  • Non-Linear Relationships: Random forest can capture non-linear relationships and complex interactions between supply chain features.

  • Feature Importance: This model provides feature importance score, helping identify influential factors associated with delivery risk.

  • Robustness: It ensembles structure which combines multiple decision trees and makes it relatively robust and capable of handling complex datasets.

CatBoost

  • Categorical Data Handling: CatBoost is particularly suitable for this project because the dataset contains numerous categorical variables such as city, countries, shipping modes, product names, and customer segment.

  • Non-Linear Relationships: CatBoost can capture complex non-linear relationships and interactions between different supply chain features.

  • Performance: CatBoost produced the best results among the evaluated models, achieving 92.03& accuracy, 92.51% F1 Score and 98.21% ROC-AUC on the stratified held out test set.

  • Reduced Preprocessing: CatBoost can process categorical variables directly reducing the need for extensive one hot encoding and making it suitable for high cardinality categorical features.

Feature Engineering

  • Domain Relevance: The selected features represent important aspects of supply chain operations including shipping configurations, order characteristics, customer information, product information, geography and time.

  • Balanced Representation: Combining operational, transactional, customer, geographic and temporal features provides a comprehensive representations of factors associated with delivery risk.

  • Temporal Features: Order date was transformed into year, month, quarter, day of week, week number and weekend indicators to capture potential seasonal and time based patterns.

Data Preprocessing

  • Data Quality: The dataset was examined for missing values, duplicate records, irrelevant variables and inconsistent information before modelling.

  • Leakage Prevention: Post outcome variables such as actual shipping duration, delivery status, shipping date and the target variable were excluded to prevent information about the delivery outcome from being directly provided to the model.

  • Train-Test Split: The dataset was divided into 80% training and 20% testing data using stratified sampling to maintain the class distributions.

SHAP EXPLAINAIBLITY

  • Objective: To explain the predictions generated by the final CatBoost model and identify the factors contributing to delivery risk predictions.

  • Method:

  • Global Explanation: Calculated mean absolute SHAP values to identify the most influential features across the dataset.

  • Individual Explanation: Examined SHAP values for individual orders to determine which features increased or decreased their predicted late delivery probability.

  • Feature Interpretation: Shipping Mode, Order City, Customer City, Type and Customer Segment were among the most influential features.

Results and Discussion

Exploratory Data Analysis — Key Drivers of Delay

Overall late-delivery rate: 54.83% of all orders in the dataset were late.

Figure 1. Order delivery outcome distribution.

Shipping Mode is the single strongest driver of delay risk: First Class shipments were late 95.3% of the time, Second Class 76.6%, Same Day 45.7%, and Standard Class only 38.1% — a striking and somewhat counterintuitive pattern in which the fastest promised service levels (First/Second Class) are associated with the highest delay rates, suggesting these tighter delivery windows leave little buffer for operational variance.

Figure 2. Late-delivery risk by shipping mode.

Scheduled shipping duration mirrors this pattern: a 1-day scheduled window carries 95.3% late risk versus 38.1% for a 4-day window, reinforcing that promised delivery speed — not distance or product type — is the dominant risk factor.

Figure 3. Late-delivery risk vs. scheduled shipping duration.

Market and Customer Segment showed only modest variation: late-delivery risk ranged narrowly from 54.4% (LATAM) to 55.2% (Europe) across markets, and from 54.7% (Corporate) to 55.1% (Home Office) across customer segments — indicating these fields are far weaker predictors than shipping configuration.

Figure 4. Late-delivery risk by customer segment.

Figure 5. Late-delivery risk by market.

A correlation and leakage screen confirmed that no numeric feature considered for modeling (order value, discount rate, product price, profit) had a meaningfully strong linear relationship with the target on its own — reinforcing that delay risk in this dataset is driven primarily by categorical shipping/geography interactions rather than transaction economics, which is exactly the kind of signal CatBoost is built to exploit.

Supervised Learning Performance Metrics

Metric Logistic Regression Random Forest CatBoost (Final)
Accuracy 0.7116 0.6922 0.9203
Precision 0.7826 0.7972 0.9537
Recall 0.6564 0.5881 0.8983
F1 Score 0.7140 0.6769 0.9251
ROC-AUC 0.7791 0.7630 0.9821

CatBoost decisively outperformed both baselines across every metric. The Logistic Regression baseline performed reasonably (ROC-AUC 0.779) given how linearly separable shipping mode and scheduled-duration risk are on their own. Random Forest, despite being a non-linear ensemble, actually underperformed the linear baseline on accuracy and F1 (0.6922 / 0.6769 vs. 0.7116 / 0.7140) — a direct consequence of one-hot encoding blowing the categorical feature space out to 5,041 columns, which diluted the signal contained in high-cardinality fields like Customer City and Order City. CatBoost's native handling of those same categorical fields — without one-hot expansion — allowed it to fully exploit that signal, achieving 92.0% accuracy and a 0.982 ROC-AUC, comfortably clearing the project's original 75% accuracy target.

The model correctly identified 17,782 of 19,796 truly late orders (89.8% recall) while keeping false alarms low (864 on-time orders incorrectly flagged, a 5.3% false-positive rate on the on-time class) — a balance well-suited to a proactive-intervention use case, where missing a genuinely late order is more costly than occasionally double-checking one that turns out fine.

Feature Importance & Explainability (SHAP)

Both CatBoost's native feature-importance ranking and SHAP's mean-|value| ranking agreed on the same top drivers of predicted delay risk:

Rank Feature CatBoost Importance Mean |SHAP|
1 Shipping Mode 32.17 1.27
2 Order City / Customer City 12.90 / 13.26 0.91 / 0.77
3 Type (order/payment type) 17.33 0.70
4 Customer Segment 3.35 0.34
5 Days for shipment (scheduled) 5.92 0.11

Shipping Mode is overwhelmingly the dominant predictor of delay risk — consistent with the EDA finding that First/Second Class shipments are far more likely to be late than Standard Class. Geographic fields (Order City, Customer City) rank second, capturing regional fulfillment and carrier-network effects that a purely categorical "Market" or "Region" field is too coarse to reflect. Order Type (the payment/transaction type field) was a surprisingly strong third-ranked driver, and scheduled shipping duration — while important in the univariate EDA — contributes comparatively little incremental signal once shipping mode is already known, since the two fields are closely linked in this dataset.

Figure 8. Top 15 CatBoost feature importances.

Figure 9. Mean |SHAP value| — top 15 features.

At the individual-order level, SHAP waterfall explanations let the model justify a specific prediction feature-by-feature. For example, one sampled test order was predicted 59.4% likely to be late (MEDIUM risk), driven primarily by a negative (risk-reducing) contribution from its Standard Class shipping mode (SHAP -0.90) partially offset by positive (risk-increasing) contributions from its customer-state and order-region values — demonstrating that the model's reasoning can be audited order-by-order, not just trusted as a black box.

Shipment Risk Prioritization

Using the CatBoost probability output, every order can be assigned to an actionable risk tier:

Late Probability Risk Tier
< 30% 🟢 LOW
30% – 60% 🟡 MEDIUM
60% – 80% 🟠 HIGH
≥ 80% 🔴 CRITICAL

This tiering was wrapped in a reusable predict_new_order() function (and a companion interactive widget interface) that accepts a new order's 28 prediction-time features and returns a probability, a risk tier, and — via explain_new_order() — the top SHAP-ranked factors behind that specific prediction. This turns the model from a static evaluation exercise into a deployable decision-support tool a logistics analyst could query for any order at creation time.

Conclusion

The implemented solution effectively combines data processing, feature engineering and supervised learning to analyse and predict delivery performance using historical supply chain data. By using appropriate preprocessing techniques and selecting relevant shipping, customer, product, geographic and temporal features the analysis identifies patterns associated with late delivery risk. The methods chosen are suitable for the nature of the data and the objectives of the analysis, providing valuable insights into supply chain operations and delivery performances.

For supervised learning Logistic Regression, Random Forest and CatBoost were employed to predict late deliveries. Logistic Regression served as a reliable baseline, offering interpretability and efficiency for classification. Random Forest was used to capture non-linear relationships and complex features interactions. CatBoost on the other hand demonstrated superior performance effectively handling the large number of categorical features present in the dataset. It’s strong predictive performance achieving 92.03% accuracy, 92.51% F1 Score and 98.21% ROC-AUC making it the selected final model.
